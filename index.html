Out of Focus?

The rise, fall and comeback of the digital camera

By Jayden de Kauwe · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · October 2026

In the 1990s, a camera meant film: 24 or 36 shots a roll, a trip to the chemist to get it developed, and no way of knowing how a photo turned out until you picked up the prints.

Chapter 1

Film to digital

For decades, cameras meant film. Sales peaked at 36.7 million film cameras in 1997, just as the first consumer digital cameras were reaching shops. Within five years digital had overtaken film, and by 2007 film cameras had all but disappeared.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), film and digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). CIPA stopped reporting film cameras after 2007, and the 1000 Cameras Dataset covers models released between 1994 and 2007.

Digital took away the wait and the cost of film. Once every shot was free and you could check it straight away on the screen, shoppers started comparing a new number on the box: megapixels.

Chapter 2

The megapixel race

As digital cameras took off, makers competed on one number: megapixels. In ten years the typical new camera went from under one megapixel to eight, while yearly sales grew to 100 million.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), total digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Megapixels are rounded to the nearest whole number in the source data. Hover over a dot to see the camera.

With sales climbing towards 100 million cameras a year, brands crowded the shelves. In 2003 alone, 18 different brands released new models, each trying to win the race.

Chapter 3

The brand wars

As the market boomed, brands raced to fill shop shelves. Olympus, Canon and Nikon were each releasing around 20 new models a year by 2007, while late arrival Samsung went from its first models in 2002 to 21 in 2007. Others, like Agfa, Toshiba and Kyocera, stopped releasing new models.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), total digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Shows 1999–2007, the years covered by both sources. Hover over a square to see the number of models.

Japanese brands, made everywhere

Almost every brand in the race was Japanese. Yet today, only 6.8% of the cameras Australia imports come from Japan, while 45.4% come from China, a country with no brands on this list. The brands stayed Japanese; the factories moved.

Changing places

The lead changed hands again and again. Kodak, America's photography giant, started 1999 releasing the most models but slid to eighth by 2007. Sony led from 2001 to 2003, Canon took over in 2004, Olympus led in 2005 and 2006, and newcomer Samsung jumped from tenth to first in a single year, 2006 to 2007.
