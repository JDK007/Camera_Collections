In the 1990s, a camera meant film: 24 or 36 shots a roll, a trip to the chemist to get it developed, and no way of knowing how a photo turned out until you picked up the prints.

Chapter 1

Film to digital

Digital cameras weren't new: a Kodak engineer built the first one in 1975. But it took until the late 1990s for them to become cheap enough for ordinary shoppers, and once they did, film collapsed far faster than it had grown, overtaken within five years of its 1997 peak. Kodak, the company that invented the digital camera, filed for bankruptcy in 2012.

Sources: CIPA, film and digital camera shipments by its member companies (most of the market); 1000 Cameras Dataset, 1994–2007. CIPA stopped reporting film cameras after 2007.

Digital took away the wait and the cost of film. Once every shot was free and you could check it straight away on the screen, shoppers started comparing a new number on the box: megapixels.

Chapter 2

The megapixel race

Megapixels were easy to print on a box and easy to compare, so they became the main way cameras were sold. Makers kept adding more, even though sensor size and lens quality matter just as much to how a photo looks. Each jump also made last year's camera feel out of date, which kept people upgrading as sales climbed towards 100 million a year.

Source: 1000 Cameras Dataset; sales figures in the text from CIPA. Megapixels are rounded to whole numbers. Use the menu to highlight a brand; hover over a dot to see the camera.

With sales climbing towards 100 million cameras a year, brands crowded the shelves. In 2003 alone, 18 different brands released new models, each trying to win the race.

Chapter 3

The brand wars

Digital turned cameras into electronics, and that opened the door to companies that had never sold a film camera. Electronics giants like Sony, Samsung, Panasonic and Casio crowded in beside the traditional camera makers, while older names like Agfa and Kyocera (maker of Contax) dropped out. This chapter covers the boom years up to 2007, the latest year with model-by-model data.

Source: 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Shows 1999–2007, the same years as the rankings below. Hover over a square to see the number of models.

Changing places

No brand stayed on top for long, because a hit camera could be out of date within a year. Kodak led in 1999 on the strength of its famous name, but it relied on film for most of its profits and slid down the ranks as film died. The electronics companies thrived instead: Sony led for three years running, and Samsung, whose first models in the dataset appear in 2002, finished 2007 in first place. Apart from Kodak and Samsung, a Japanese brand led every year.

Sources: 1000 Cameras Dataset; country is where each company has its headquarters. Ties are ordered alphabetically, and a line starts in the brand's first year in the dataset. Click a line to highlight it, double-click to reset.

Then the camera moved into everyone's pocket. As smartphones got better at taking photos, many people stopped carrying a separate camera at all, and the cheap point-and-shoot lost its reason to exist.

Chapter 4

Crash and comeback

The iPhone arrived in 2007, and within a few years most people carried a good camera in their pocket. The cheap point-and-shoot had nothing extra to offer, so it took almost all of the 92% fall from the 2010 peak. Cameras with swappable lenses held on because they still do what phones can't: real zoom lenses, better photos in low light, and sharp shots of fast-moving action.

Source: CIPA, digital camera shipments by its member companies (most of the market). Interchangeable-lens cameras are counted from 2003. Hover over a band for details.

From mirrors to mirrorless

A DSLR shows the scene through a mirror; a mirrorless camera drops the mirror and shows a live preview on a screen, exactly as the photo will look, which is what phone users had come to expect. Without the mirror, cameras also became smaller, lighter and better at video. The switch sped up after 2018, when Canon and Nikon, the two biggest DSLR makers, both launched full-frame mirrorless cameras.

Source: CIPA, shipments by its member companies; DSLR and mirrorless reported separately from 2012. Each square is 1 in every 100 interchangeable-lens cameras.

From bargain to premium

Earlier in this chapter we saw how many cameras were sold; this chart shows what they cost. Prices fell while makers fought for volume, but once phones took over the cheap end of the market, makers stopped competing on price and moved to higher-end models with bigger sensors and better lenses. The average compact now costs more than twice its 2003 price, not because the same camera got dearer, but because the cheap ones are no longer made.

Source: CIPA, average price per camera shipped, in yen, not adjusted for inflation. Hover over a point for the price.

Chapter 5

Where Australia's cameras come from

Camera brands design their products at home but build them wherever manufacturing is cheapest, and for most electronics that has been China. In 2025 China supplied 45% of the value of Australia's camera imports, about A$637 million, even though none of the brands from Chapter 3 come from there, while Japan, home to 16 of those 21 brands, supplied just 7%. Drag the slider back to 2017 and Japan was the second-biggest supplier, with 18%. The brands stayed Japanese; the factories moved.

Source: UN Comtrade, value of Australian camera imports by country, top ten each year, 2017–2025 (HS 852589; HS 852580 before 2022). Includes video and security cameras. Drag the slider to change year; hover over a line for details.

Cheap from China, premium from the brands' home countries

Value per camera reveals two different trades sharing one category. China ships millions of low-cost cameras, likely including security, dash and web cameras, at about A$130 each. Japan, the USA and Germany send far fewer, at A$760 to A$1,900 each: the high-end cameras and gear that the brands still make closest to home.

Sources: UN Comtrade, Australian camera imports, top ten countries, 2025 (HS 852589, includes video and security cameras, so averages are lower than typical camera prices); brand headquarters from the 1000 Cameras Dataset. Circles have a minimum size so small suppliers stay visible. Hover over a circle for details.

Back from the brink

After hitting bottom in 2023, the camera market is growing again, and Australia's camera imports rose 30% in value from 2022 to 2025. Less than half of that rise came from China. Electronics makers have been spreading production into countries like Vietnam, Thailand and Mexico so they rely less on a single country, and it shows: imports from Vietnam rose 82%, Mexico's more than doubled, and Myanmar went from almost nothing to A$21 million.

Source: UN Comtrade, value of Australian camera imports by country, 2022 and 2025 (HS 852589, includes video and security cameras). Each bar is that country's change as a share of the 2022 total, so the bars add up to the total rise.

Chapter 6

A nation of camera buyers

Australia makes very few cameras of its own, so what it imports is a good measure of what Australians buy. Per person, that makes Australia one of the world's biggest camera markets: about A$40 of imported cameras for every Australian in 2024, more than Canada (A$36), Switzerland (A$31) or the United States (A$28). The countries that make cameras, like China, Thailand and Taiwan, sell far more than they buy.

Sources: UN Comtrade, camera imports minus exports by country, 2024 (HS 852589, includes video and security cameras); World Bank, population, 2024. An average across everyone, not what each person spent. Tiny places like Macao rank higher but are too small to show; grey means no data.

Who uses them?

Part of that demand comes from people who take photos for a living, from wedding and event photographers to real-estate and product photographers. It is mostly solo work: three in four of Australia's 9,400 photography businesses have no staff. They are most common in New South Wales and Victoria, about 39 for every 100,000 people, where larger cities likely create more paid work, and least common in the Northern Territory (17).

Sources: ABS, Counts of Australian Businesses (Professional Photographic Services), June 2025; ABS, state and territory population, June 2025. Hover over a state for details.

Glossary

Camera shipments
Cameras sent by makers to shops and distributors. This is the usual way the industry counts sales.
CIPA
Camera & Imaging Products Association, the Japanese industry body whose members (Canon, Nikon, Sony and others) make most of the world's cameras. Its figures cover its members only.
Compact camera
A point-and-shoot camera with a built-in lens that can't be removed.
Interchangeable-lens camera
A camera whose lens can be swapped for another, such as a DSLR or mirrorless camera.
DSLR
Digital single-lens reflex: a camera that uses a mirror to show the scene through an optical viewfinder.
Mirrorless camera
An interchangeable-lens camera without the mirror, which shows the scene on a small screen instead. It is smaller and lighter than a DSLR.
Megapixel (MP)
One million tiny dots (pixels) that make up a digital photo. More megapixels means more detail.
Typical camera
The middle value (median): half the cameras released that year had fewer megapixels and half had more.
Percentage points
The difference between two percentages. In the waterfall chart, each country's points add up to the total rise.
HS 852589
The trade code customs offices use for cameras. It covers digital cameras as well as video and security cameras.
Imports per person
The value of cameras a country imports, minus those it exports, divided by its population.
