Out of Focus?

The rise, fall and comeback of the digital camera

In the 1990s, a camera meant film: 24 or 36 shots a roll, a trip to the chemist to get it developed, and no way of knowing how a photo turned out until you picked up the prints.

Chapter 1

Film to digital

For decades, cameras meant film. Sales peaked at 36.7 million film cameras in 1997, just as the first consumer digital cameras were reaching shops. Within five years digital had overtaken film, and by 2007 film cameras had all but disappeared.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), film and digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). CIPA stopped reporting film cameras after 2007, and the 1000 Cameras Dataset covers models released between 1994 and 2007.

Digital took away the wait and the cost of film. Once every shot was free and you could check it straight away on the screen, shoppers started comparing a new number on the box: megapixels.

Chapter 2

The megapixel race

As digital cameras took off, makers competed on one number: megapixels. In ten years the typical new camera went from under one megapixel to eight, while yearly sales grew to 100 million.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), total digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Megapixels are rounded to the nearest whole number in the source data. Hover over a dot to see the camera.

With sales climbing towards 100 million cameras a year, brands crowded the shelves. In 2003 alone, 18 different brands released new models, each trying to win the race.

Chapter 3

The brand wars

As the market boomed, brands raced to fill shop shelves. Olympus, Canon and Nikon were each releasing around 20 new models a year by 2007, while late arrival Samsung went from its first models in 2002 to 21 in 2007. Others, like Agfa, Toshiba and Kyocera, stopped releasing new models.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), total digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market; 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Shows 1999–2007, the years covered by both sources. Hover over a square to see the number of models.

Japanese brands, made everywhere

Almost every brand in the race was Japanese. Yet today, only 6.8% of the cameras Australia imports come from Japan, while 45.4% come from China, a country with no brands on this list. The brands stayed Japanese; the factories moved.

Changing places

The lead changed hands again and again. Kodak, America's photography giant, started 1999 releasing the most models but slid to eighth by 2007. Sony led from 2001 to 2003, Canon took over in 2004, Olympus led in 2005 and 2006, and newcomer Samsung jumped from tenth to first in a single year, 2006 to 2007.

Source: 1000 Cameras Dataset (Isenberg, Dragicevic & Jansen, via Kaggle). Ranks compare the ten brands with the most models in the dataset; brands with the same number of models are ordered alphabetically. Click a line to highlight a brand, double-click to reset. Hover over a point for details.

Then the camera moved into everyone's pocket. As smartphones got better at taking photos, many people stopped carrying a separate camera at all, and the cheap point-and-shoot lost its reason to exist.

Chapter 4

Crash and comeback

Camera shipments peaked at 121.5 million in 2010, then fell 92% to 9.4 million by 2025. Cheap compact cameras took almost all of the fall: 110 million were shipped in 2008, but just 2.4 million in 2025. Cameras with swappable lenses held on far better, and in 2018 they outsold compacts for the first time.

Source: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), digital camera shipments by its member companies (e.g. Canon, Nikon, Sony), which make up most of the market. Interchangeable-lens cameras (DSLR and mirrorless) are counted from 2003. The width of each band shows how many cameras were shipped. Hover over a band for details.

From mirrors to mirrorless

The cameras that survived changed on the inside too. A DSLR uses a mirror to show the scene in the viewfinder; a mirrorless camera drops the mirror and shows the scene on a small screen instead, which makes it smaller and lighter. In 2012, 8 in every 10 interchangeable-lens cameras shipped were DSLRs. Mirrorless took the lead in 2020, and by 2025 it was 9 in 10.

Source: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), shipments by its member companies. DSLR and mirrorless cameras are reported separately from 2012. Each square stands for 1 in every 100 interchangeable-lens cameras shipped. Hover over a square for details.

Back from the brink

After hitting bottom in 2023, camera shipments are rising again, up 18% worldwide since 2022. Australians are part of the comeback: over the same years, the value of cameras imported into Australia rose 30%.

Sources: Camera & Imaging Products Association (CIPA), digital camera shipments by its member companies; UN Comtrade, total value of Australian imports of cameras (HS 852589, which also includes video and security cameras), 2022–2025, the years available. Changes are measured from 2022. Hover over a point for details.

Chapter 5

Where Australia's cameras come from

In 2025, Australia imported A$1.4 billion worth of cameras. China supplied 45% of that value, about A$637 million, even though none of the brands from Chapter 3 come from China. Japan, home to 16 of those 21 brands, supplied just 7%.

Sources: UN Comtrade, value of Australian imports of cameras (HS 852589, which also includes video and security cameras) by country of origin, 2025, converted from US dollars at the 2025 average rate of A$1 = US$0.6449 (Australian Taxation Office, using Reserve Bank of Australia rates). Comtrade lists Taiwan as "Other Asia". Circles show where the 21 brands in the 1000 Cameras Dataset have their headquarters. Hover over a country for details.

Cheap from China, premium from Germany

Not every camera is worth the same. Divide each country's import value by the number of cameras and a pattern appears: cameras from China averaged about A$130 each, those from Japan about A$760, and those from Germany about A$1,900.

Source: UN Comtrade, Australian imports of cameras (HS 852589), 2025, for the ten countries with the highest import value. Average value per camera is the import value divided by the number of units; because the category also includes low-cost security and video cameras, these averages are lower than typical camera prices. Chapter 6

A global market

Camera makers shipped 9.4 million cameras worldwide in 2025. The Americas and China took the most, and every region except Japan bought more than in 2024. Australia followed the trend: the value of its camera imports rose 14% in the same year.
